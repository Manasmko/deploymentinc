Deployment 01Site: Ad ops, SEAStatus: Production, v23+Asked to build it: No

The post-campaign report engine

Internal forward deployment, before I knew that was a job title
The mess

Every campaign ended with the same tax: pull the numbers, retype them into a deck, write the insight, export. One to two hours a report, entirely manual, and I was accountable for 150+ of them across Southeast Asia.

The call

The obvious fix was a better template. The real constraint was that data assembly and thinking were tangled — the analyst couldn't start reasoning until the mechanical work was done. So I automated assembly and left judgment with the human.

Full log — architecture, validation, adoption
The second call, which mattered more

Browser-based. No install, no new system to log into. Harder to build and much harder to make robust — but the alternative was a beautiful internal tool with four users. Adoption was the constraint I designed against, not speed.

What made it survive

A token-substitution build pipeline so the source template stays clean and rebuildable across format variants, and a validation layer — syntax checks, tag-balance parsing, leftover-token detection — that catches errors before anything reaches a client. Insights run through a server-side proxy rather than exposing keys in the browser.

It later extended to three ad formats, each with its own metrics, benchmarks and insight logic: standard display, connected TV, and rich media engagement.

Adoption

It's now the standard way post-campaign reporting gets produced on the team — not a prototype somebody demoed once, but a tool other people's work depends on.

What I'd do differently

I built most of it before showing anyone, and got lucky. A rough version in front of two account managers in week one would have found what actually broke months earlier — twenty-three versions is a number I'm not entirely proud of.

Deployment 02Site: Ad ops, SEACoverage: 150–200 live campaignsRuns: Daily, unattended

The morning sweep agent

Proof the first one wasn't a fluke
The mess

Every morning somebody read 150 to 200 live campaigns by hand to find the ones falling behind. One to two hours of the day before any actual work started — and because it depended on a person remembering, it slipped exactly when things were busiest.

The call

Don't build a dashboard. A dashboard needs someone to look at it, and the failure mode here was precisely that nobody looked. Build something that runs on its own and speaks up only when something's wrong.

Full log — why an agent and not a report

The agent drives the reporting interface the way a person would, sweeps every live campaign, and compiles a single HTML digest shared across three teams. Same artifact for everyone, which removed a second problem I hadn't set out to solve — ops, account management and sales had been working from three different readings of the same numbers.

The check stopped depending on anyone's memory, which was the actual problem. The reading was never the hard part.

What I'd do differently

It drives a user interface, so it's brittle by construction — a layout change can break it silently, and silent failure is the worst possible outcome for a tool whose whole job is to speak up. Deploying this into a client's environment, I'd have pushed for API access up front rather than routing around the constraint.

Deployment 03Site: Commercial ops, multi-marketUsers: Sales + financeNext market: Indonesia

The insertion order system

The one where the hard part was legal, not technical
The mess

Insertion orders — the contract that makes a campaign real — were built by hand in Word and Excel. Which meant duplicated order numbers, inconsistent terms, and every market's legal requirements depending on somebody remembering to change a field.

The call

Treat the entity structure as the architecture, not as a set of templates. Get that right and a new market is configuration. Get it wrong and every new market is a rebuild.

Full log — the entity model

Sales and finance log into the same system. Selecting an agency pulls its stored details; selecting a market applies that entity's legal terms and numbering series; campaign details go in once and the completed order renders to PDF ready to send.

The real test of the architecture was whether adding a second market cost weeks or days. It's the deployment I'd point to for whether I can build something meant to be handed to people I'll never meet.

What I'd do differently

I designed the entity model from the two markets I could see. I should have sat with the commercial lead for the third before writing any of it — the cost of guessing wrong on that layer is far higher than on anything built above it.

Deployment 04Site: Avadh Vidya Private ITI, Ballia, UPScope: Full operational chargeOwned: The P&L

The one where I owned the number, not the artifact

Two years · four trades · 100+ students
The mess

A vocational institute in eastern Uttar Pradesh, losing money. The instinct in the room was that it was an awareness problem — not enough people know about us, so spend more on reaching them.

The call

It wasn't a top-of-funnel problem. The leak was inside admissions itself. Spending more on awareness would have poured people into a process already losing them — so I held marketing flat and rebuilt admissions first.

Full log — the people part, and the software that came after
What it actually took

Restructuring how enquiries were handled and converted, and managing both formal and non-formal staff through a change none of them had asked for. This part had no code in it at all. It was standing in a room in Ballia persuading people who had done it the same way for years.

Then — and only then — the software

The fixed process needed a system to hold it, so I built the institute's ERP: admissions, a fee ledger, a payroll engine with sandwich-leave rules, two-tier permissions and student tracking. Next.js, TypeScript, Drizzle, Postgres. Designed, built and shipped alone.

The sequence is the point. I fixed the business first and wrote the software second. Doing it the other way round is the most common way this kind of project quietly fails.

What I'd do differently

I under-communicated in the first two months. I had the diagnosis before the staff had any reason to trust it, and I treated their resistance as an obstacle rather than as information. The turnaround would have been faster if I'd spent the first three weeks listening instead of restructuring.

The second question

Why India's first AI FDE company matters to me

You asked for this in the post. It deserves more than a line, so here it is properly.

The bottleneck for AI in Indian companies isn't the models. It's that the people who understand the problem can't specify the solution, and the people who can build it have never watched the problem happen.

I've been on both sides of that gap.

At an ad-tech company I watched teams describe what they needed and receive software that matched the description exactly and solved nothing — because the thing that was actually slow was never the thing anyone thought to mention. The tools that worked were the ones where somebody sat in the chair, did the job badly for a week, and then built. That isn't a methodology I read about. It's the only thing I've seen work.

At the institute I've seen the other end. A real business, with real inefficiency, in a town nobody is flying into to fix. The Indian mid-market is thousands of businesses like that one. They don't need a platform. They need someone to show up, understand what they do all day, and build into it.

Product-shaped software assumes the buyer can specify. Most of this market can't yet — and someone has to be the reference implementation.

That's not a gap in the software. It's a gap in deployment, and it's a category that doesn't exist here yet.
